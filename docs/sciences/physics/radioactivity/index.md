Atomic Structure & Isotopes
- Atoms consist of a nucleus (protons + neutrons) and orbiting electrons.
- Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
- Most of an atom’s mass is in the nucleus; the radius of the nucleus is much smaller than the atom.

☢️ Types of Radiation
- Alpha (α): 2 protons + 2 neutrons (helium nucleus); highly ionising, low penetration (stopped by paper).
- Beta-minus (β⁻): a neutron turns into a proton + electron; medium ionising, penetrates paper but stopped by aluminium.
- Beta-plus (β⁺): a proton turns into a neutron + positron.
- Gamma (γ): electromagnetic wave; low ionising, high penetration (needs thick lead to stop).
- Neutron radiation: emitted in some nuclear reactions.

🔄 Radioactive Decay
- Random process: cannot predict when a nucleus will decay.
- Decay equations: must conserve mass and charge.
- Nuclear rearrangement: often emits gamma radiation after decay.

📉 Activity & Half-Life
- Activity: rate of decay, measured in Becquerels (Bq).
- Half-life: time for half the radioactive nuclei to decay.
- Used to calculate remaining activ- ity or undecayed nuclei over time.

🌍 Background Radiation
- Comes from:
- Natural sources: rocks, cosmic rays, radon gas.
- Man-made sources: medical uses, nuclear industry.
- Measured using Geiger–Müller tubes or photographic film.

🧪 Uses of Radiation
- Medical: PET scans, cancer treatment (radiotherapy), sterilising equipment.
- Industrial: smoke detectors (alpha), thickness control (beta), tracing leaks.
- Food: irradiation to kill bacteria.

⚠️ Dangers & Safety
- Ionising radiation can damage DNA, cause mutations, cancer.
- Contamination: radioactive material on/in body.
- Irradiation: exposure to radiation without contact.
- Precautions: shielding, limiting exposure time, distance, protective clothing.

⚛️ Nuclear Fission & Fusion
- Fission: splitting of large nuclei (e.g. U-235), releases energy + neutrons → chain reaction.
- Fusion: joining of small nuclei (e.g. hydrogen in stars), releases more energy than fission, but requires high temperature and pressure.
